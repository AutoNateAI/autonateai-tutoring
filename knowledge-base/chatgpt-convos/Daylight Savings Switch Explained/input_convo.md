Time itself doesn’t change. Humans just move the labels on the clock. Civilization basically agreed to lie to itself twice a year so the sun lines up better with our schedules. Strange species we are. 🕰️
The “Sunday vs Monday” thing
Your girlfriend’s mom is remembering something that sounds plausible, but historically it’s a little off.
Daylight Saving Time (DST) in the United States has almost always switched on Sunday, not Monday.
There was confusion decades ago when the exact date of the switch kept changing. People occasionally showed up early or late because they forgot the date—but the Sunday timing itself wasn't changed to fix lateness.
The modern schedule came from the Energy Policy Act of 2005, which took effect in 2007. That law moved the DST season to:
* Start: Second Sunday in March
* End: First Sunday in November
Before 2007 it was:
* First Sunday in April → last Sunday in October.
So the day stayed Sunday; Congress just stretched the DST season longer.
Why Sunday? Simple logistics:
* 2:00 AM Sunday is when the fewest trains, flights, banks, and businesses are operating.
* Changing clocks then causes the least disruption.
Transportation planners love boring hours like that.
Who actually has the power?
In the U.S., the authority sits with Congress.
The original national system came from the Uniform Time Act of 1966, passed by the United States Congress and signed by Lyndon B. Johnson.
That law standardized time zones and DST across the country.
States have limited freedom:
* A state can opt out of DST entirely.
* But they cannot create their own DST schedule without federal approval.
That’s why:
* Arizona (except Navajo Nation) doesn't observe DST.
* Hawaii doesn't either.
But if a state wants permanent daylight saving time, Congress must approve it.
Why DST existed in the first place
The core idea was energy savings.
It was first widely used during World War I and later World War II to conserve fuel by aligning daylight with working hours.
Modern research suggests the energy savings are tiny or inconsistent. Some studies even show increases because of air conditioning use.
So the whole thing survives mostly because:
* habit
* nationwide coordination inertia
* political gridlock
The modern political fight
There’s a movement to eliminate clock switching entirely.
One proposal, the Sunshine Protection Act, would make DST permanent.
It has passed the Senate before but stalled in the House.
Meanwhile 20+ states have passed laws saying they want permanent DST if Congress allows it.
The deeper weirdness
The real cosmic punchline:
Time zones themselves are artificial. Before railroads in the 1800s, every town ran on local solar time. Noon meant the sun was overhead in that town. The U.S. had hundreds of local times.
Railroads couldn’t run a continent like that, so in 1883 they imposed standardized time zones in one sweep.
Civilization literally snapped the clock grid over the planet like graph paper over a sphere. 🌍
Human timekeeping is basically a giant synchronization protocol.
The interesting frontier now is that AI systems, distributed computing, and global markets run on UTC timestamps, not local clocks. Machines already escaped the daylight saving ritual.
Humans are the ones still arguing with the wall clock.
