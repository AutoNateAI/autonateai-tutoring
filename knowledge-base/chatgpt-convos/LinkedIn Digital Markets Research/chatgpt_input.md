Heat does something fascinating to the brain, and the sauna–exercise loop you’re doing at the YMCA happens to hit several of the mechanisms researchers study when they look at creativity, mood, and cognitive resilience. None of this is mystical; it’s physiology interacting with attention. The body is basically running a controlled stress experiment on itself.
Start with the simple part: heat is a mild stressor. When you sit in a sauna or hot tub, your core temperature rises slightly. The brain interprets that the same way it interprets moderate exercise—“something is happening, adapt.” Heart rate climbs, blood vessels dilate, and circulation increases. That increased circulation sends more oxygenated blood to the brain. Studies on sauna exposure show heart rates often reach 100–150 beats per minute, similar to light cardio.
That matters because the brain likes blood flow. Regions involved in executive function—planning, strategy, and storytelling—tend to become more metabolically active when circulation increases.
Then a deeper mechanism kicks in: heat shock proteins.
Cells have a fascinating defense system. When temperature rises, cells produce molecules called heat shock proteins. Their job is basically to protect and repair other proteins inside cells that get damaged under stress. In the brain, this protection helps neurons maintain stability and resilience. Researchers studying sauna exposure have suggested this cellular repair system may partly explain why frequent sauna use correlates with lower rates of certain neurodegenerative diseases in long-term population studies.
There’s a famous Finnish cohort study—over 2,300 men followed for about 20 years—showing that people who used saunas 4–7 times per week had about a 60–65% lower risk of Alzheimer’s disease compared with those who used them once a week. Correlation doesn’t prove causation, but it strongly hints that repeated heat exposure trains protective systems in the brain.
Now layer in exercise, which you’re doing before the sauna.
Exercise increases a molecule called BDNF — Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor. Think of BDNF as fertilizer for neurons. It encourages the growth of new connections and strengthens existing ones. The hippocampus—an area deeply involved in memory and imagination—responds strongly to BDNF.
When you lift weights or do cardio, BDNF rises. When you add sauna afterwards, circulation stays elevated and recovery signals continue firing. The combination of exercise → heat → relaxation creates a neurochemical cocktail where the brain is unusually plastic and receptive.
Another piece is endorphins and endocannabinoids.
Heat exposure increases both. Endorphins are natural opioids the body releases under stress; they create the calm, almost euphoric feeling many people experience after a sauna. Endocannabinoids—chemicals similar to those activated by cannabis—also increase with heat and exercise. These molecules are linked to the “runner’s high,” but they also quiet activity in the brain’s fear circuits.
When fear quiets down, creativity goes up. The brain shifts away from threat monitoring and toward exploratory thinking.
Then there’s the attentional shift.
Inside a sauna, sensory input drops dramatically. No phone. No scrolling. No constant novelty. Just heat, breathing, and body awareness. In neuroscience terms, this tends to increase activity in the default mode network, a system involved in narrative thinking, memory recall, and imagining the future.
That network is the same one activated when:
* you daydream
* you mentally rehearse future scenarios
* you build stories about your life
So when you sit there telling yourself the story of Nathan Baker building workshops, selling seats, growing a community—you’re actually using a network the brain evolved for simulating possible futures.
Elite performers use a similar trick deliberately. It’s called prospective simulation or mental rehearsal. The brain partially activates the same neural pathways used when performing the real action.
In other words, if you vividly imagine running a workshop—walking into the room, teaching engineers, answering questions—the motor and planning circuits begin mapping the behavior before it happens.
Your sauna routine amplifies that because the brain is already in a relaxed but alert state.
