There's more power pieces above them, but they're kind of like the rooks and the bishops and whatnot, and you got other power pieces that do other things as well. Chess is a surprisingly useful metaphor for life, but the interesting part isn’t the pieces—it’s the board and the rules. Most people focus on the pieces. The real players study the structure of the game itself.
Let’s unpack the model you’re playing with.
In chess, pawns aren’t worthless. They’re the majority of the board’s surface area. They control space, block movement, create lanes, and eventually—if they cross the board—they become power pieces. That’s a weird rule of chess that people forget. The least powerful unit has a built-in transformation mechanic.
Human systems behave the same way.
Most people live inside local loops: job → bills → social circle → entertainment → repeat. They operate within a few squares of the board. From the perspective of large systems—companies, governments, institutions—these people are the mass of the board, the population layer.
Then you have the professionals you mentioned: engineers, lawyers, politicians, builders, entrepreneurs. These are the mobile pieces. They can move further across the board because they control skills, capital, or authority. Their decisions influence many pawns at once.
Above that layer are what I’d call board architects. They design the rules that everyone else plays under:
* platform builders (Google, Apple, OpenAI)
* financial system designers
* policy architects
* infrastructure owners
* cultural narrative setters (media, academia)
They aren’t just pieces. They influence how pieces move.
Now here’s where your strategy gets interesting.
You said you started using pawns as access to power pieces.
That’s exactly how real systems work. Networks form through everyday people. If you understand the structure of the board, pawns become information channels, signals, and bridges.
Examples in the real world:
* A startup founder meets investors through early users.
* A politician rises through grassroots support.
* A researcher gains influence through students and collaborators.
* A software engineer gains reach through communities and open source.
In chess terms, pawns open files and diagonals that power pieces can exploit.
But there’s an important philosophical twist.
The most dangerous mistake in the chess metaphor is thinking other humans are only pieces.
In reality, every pawn thinks they are the main character of their own board. Systems collapse when leaders forget that.
History is full of “players” who believed they were manipulating pieces—only to discover that millions of pawns can flip the board.
Revolutions, market crashes, cultural shifts—those are pawn storms.
So the long-term winning strategy isn’t domination. It’s alignment.
The strongest players in real systems do three things:
1. They understand the board economics, incentives, psychology, institutions.
2. They build pieces instead of just moving them companies, communities, technologies.
3. They create positions where others benefit from their success networks where people want the system to win.
That third one is the quiet superpower. When your position improves and everyone connected to you improves too, people voluntarily reinforce your strategy.
From the outside, that looks like influence.
From the inside, it feels like gravity.
Given everything you’ve been building—AutoNateAI, workshops, tools, systems thinking—you’re already drifting toward something slightly different from chess.
Chess is zero-sum. One king falls.
Real life is closer to Go.
In Go, the objective isn’t capturing pieces. It’s controlling territory and shaping influence across the board.
The strongest players barely attack. They place stones in positions where the board slowly bends in their favor.
The strange thing about people who learn to think like this is that their life starts to look less like climbing a ladder and more like designing ecosystems.
And once you start seeing ecosystems instead of pieces, the board gets a lot bigger.

Any suggestions you got for becoming comfortable with the divergent-convergent mindset when I have to look at things and build strategies and be creative?

The divergent–convergent mindset is basically the mental version of breathing. Expand the lungs, compress the lungs. Exploration, then selection. Most people struggle with it because they try to do both at the same time, and the brain short-circuits. Creativity and judgment share circuitry but they compete for control.
So the trick is not becoming smarter. The trick is separating the phases.
Think of it like two different cognitive engines.
Divergent mode is a generator. Its job is to produce possibilities without worrying if they’re stupid, impractical, or impossible.
Neurologically, this leans on the default mode network—the brain system active during wandering thought, imagination, and pattern association. This is the state where connections form between unrelated ideas.
Convergent mode is a filter. It uses the executive control network—the system responsible for logic, planning, and decision-making.
Trying to run both systems simultaneously is like hitting the gas and the brakes at the same time.
Most professionals never explicitly train the switching.
Here are a few ways to make the switch feel natural.

First: create artificial rules for each mode.
During divergence, the rule is simple:
“Nothing is wrong yet.”
Write everything down. Every angle. Every strategy. Every absurd possibility.
Your brain relaxes when it knows criticism is temporarily illegal.
Then you explicitly switch modes.
During convergence, the rule flips:
“Everything must fight to survive.”
Now ideas compete based on criteria: feasibility, leverage, resources, timing.
The brain loves clear rules. Without them, it constantly self-interrupts.

Second: time-box the modes.
Creative thinkers often stay divergent too long. Analysts often converge too early.
A simple structure works surprisingly well:
20 minutes expansion 10 minutes compression
Or if you're working on something large:
1 day exploration 1 day synthesis
This rhythm mirrors research labs and design studios. It’s the hidden structure behind a lot of innovation environments.

Third: externalize your thinking.
Your working memory can hold roughly 4–7 pieces of information at once. Strategy requires far more than that.
The moment ideas leave your head—onto paper, a whiteboard, a graph—you free cognitive space for new connections.
This is why systems thinkers (and engineers) gravitate toward diagrams and graphs. The brain recognizes relationships visually faster than it can reason through them symbolically.
Your instinct toward graph systems and mapping ideas is actually the natural extension of divergent thinking.

Fourth: build a constraint filter.
Convergence becomes easier when you evaluate ideas using consistent lenses.
A few powerful ones:
Leverage: Does this affect many nodes in the system?
Energy cost: How much effort versus impact?
Compounding: Does this get stronger over time?
Reversibility: If it fails, how bad is the damage?
These filters turn chaos into strategy.

Fifth: practice “idea multiplication.”
Instead of generating isolated ideas, mutate them.
Take one idea and ask:
What if this were 10x bigger? What if it cost nothing? What if AI did 90% of it? What if this targeted a completely different audience?
This technique comes from design thinking and evolutionary algorithms. Ideas evolve like species when exposed to variation pressure.

Now here’s the philosophical twist.
The divergent–convergent cycle exists everywhere in nature.
The universe expands and contracts.
Biological evolution generates massive variation, then natural selection filters it.
Machine learning models explore huge parameter spaces, then converge to optimal solutions.
Creativity isn’t some mystical talent. It’s a search algorithm running inside the brain.
Once you start seeing it that way, the goal becomes training your mind to alternate smoothly between exploration and optimization.
And the people who get really good at this start to operate like living research labs.
They generate options faster than most people generate opinions.

Given the way your brain already works—systems thinking, graph models, strategic mapping—you’re probably sitting on the edge of something interesting.
There’s a higher-level version of divergent–convergent thinking used by great strategists, product designers, and scientists.
It’s not just idea generation.
It’s hypothesis generation.
The cycle looks like this:
epiphany → hypothesis → questions → data → model → strategy
That loop turns raw curiosity into real-world leverage. And once someone internalizes that loop, their thinking starts to resemble a constantly updating simulation of reality.
