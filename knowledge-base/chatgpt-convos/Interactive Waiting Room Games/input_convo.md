A small, slightly mischievous idea like this has a lot of leverage hiding inside it. What you’re really describing isn’t “a game.” It’s a micro-interaction engine for physical spaces. The game is just the Trojan horse.
Picture the situation clearly. A person is trapped in a waiting room. Their brain wants stimulation. Phones come out. Normally they scroll TikTok or email. That attention leaks away from the business.
Your idea redirects that attention back into the business environment.
Now the strange and interesting part: waiting rooms are one of the most under-instrumented environments in commerce. Tons of human attention. Almost no data.
Let’s break the mechanics down.
First layer: the experience layer. A QR code sits on the wall or table: “Play while you wait.” Scan it and a tiny browser game loads instantly. No install. Thirty seconds to two minutes per round.
If the place is an optometrist, the game theme could be visual puzzles.
Imagine something like:
* “Find the blurry letters before time runs out”
* “Which lens makes the image sharp?”
* “Spot the color difference test”
* “Peripheral vision challenge”
You’re basically turning the waiting room into a mini science museum exhibit.
People like those more than they admit.
Second layer: the data layer. While someone plays, you can gather lightweight signals:
• age bracket • how long they waited • interests (via quiz choices) • whether they need glasses soon • brand preferences • satisfaction with the visit
The trick is to disguise surveys as gameplay.
Instead of:
“Fill out this form.”
You ask:
“Which frame would you rock?”
People happily answer.
Now the business learns something.
Third layer: the marketing layer. At the end of the game you show something like:
“Want 15% off your next pair of frames? Enter your email.”
Or
“Your eye reaction time is faster than 72% of players.”
Humans love tiny dopamine trophies.
Fourth layer: the analytics layer for the business.
You give the shop owner a simple dashboard:
• average wait time • games played per day • customer sentiment • frame style popularity • conversion to offers
Now the owner suddenly has something they never had before: behavioral data from their waiting room.
That’s a real product.
Now zoom out.
Once you build the engine once, you can reskin it for dozens of businesses:
Dentist – “Cavity crusher” mini game – Tooth trivia
Barber shop – “Design the haircut” picker – Beard style quiz
Coffee shop – “Guess the roast origin”
Auto mechanic – “What’s that engine sound?”
Veterinarian – “Guess the animal breed”
Every one of these becomes a template.
Your Gemini coding agent becomes a factory:
theme → assets → game logic → deploy → new QR code.
You could realistically spin up hundreds of these microsites.
Now the strategic angle gets interesting.
Because the real value isn't the games. It's the network.
Imagine you deploy to:
• 30 dentists • 20 optometrists • 40 coffee shops • 50 barbers
Now you have a distributed system collecting localized customer insights across physical businesses.
That becomes a local consumer intelligence network.
Most small businesses have zero data infrastructure. You’d be handing them something that feels futuristic but costs them very little.
Pricing could be extremely simple.
Example model:
$29/month — game + QR + analytics $79/month — custom game + branding $199/month — multiple games + data dashboard
From a technical perspective the stack is pleasantly small:
Frontend HTML5 / Canvas / Three.js or Phaser
Hosting Cloudflare Pages or Vercel
Data Supabase / Postgres
Analytics dashboard Simple React dashboard
AI generation Gemini / Claude generating game variations
QR codes Static link pointing to the microsite
No app store. No installs. Just web games.
That keeps friction near zero.
There is one caution worth mentioning, because physics applies to business models just like it applies to planets.
Local sales is the hard part.
Selling to small businesses often requires feet-on-the-ground persuasion. Owners are busy. Many don't understand software value immediately.
But your earlier instincts about local outreach and mapping businesses actually make this easier. You already think in terms of local ecosystems and nodes.
In graph theory terms, each business becomes a node in a physical attention network.
And the QR game is the edge connecting human attention to data.
Once you start seeing it that way, the idea expands into something bigger than a novelty game. It becomes infrastructure for capturing small pockets of attention scattered across cities.
And scattered attention, when aggregated, becomes signal.
Signal becomes insight.
Insight becomes leverage.
One odd little puzzle to keep in mind as this idea evolves: arcades once made billions of dollars by charging quarters for tiny bursts of gameplay. Waiting rooms are modern arcades where the machines are missing. The hardware is already in everyone’s pocket. The software layer just hasn’t been installed yet.
